Large language models have emerged as essential tools for automatic evaluation due to their scalability across diverse tasks. This survey highlights the evolution of these evaluation systems from single-pass scoring to structured architectures. These advanced approaches include rubric-based calibration, multi-agent collaboration, and agentic frameworks.

Recent methods like AutoCalibrate \cite{autocalibrate} and Prometheus 2 \cite{prometheus2} demonstrate that explicit criteria and specialized models significantly improve alignment with human judgment. Similarly, multi-agent architectures \cite{prd} and culturally aware frameworks \cite{macd} prove that incorporating diverse perspectives reduces systematic bias. Furthermore, agentic frameworks \cite{agent_judge, auto_eval_judge} introduce stepwise verification and task decomposition to enable reliable assessments of complex outputs.

Despite these structural advancements, critical challenges remain. Language model judges continue to exhibit sensitivity to prompt design and superficial features \cite{comm_systems}. Cultural bias and value misalignment also persist across diverse contexts \cite{macd}. While multi-agent debate systems appear promising, they do not guarantee genuine reasoning and often suffer from false consensus \cite{debate_limit}. Additionally, integrating external tools introduces novel vulnerabilities like tool-memory conflicts \cite{tool_conflict}. From a deployment perspective, these advanced pipelines substantially increase computational cost, latency, and implementation complexity \cite{agent_survey}.

The surveyed literature suggests that no single mitigation strategy fully resolves these evaluation limits. The most promising direction lies in hybrid architectures. These systems must combine calibrated rubrics, structured reasoning, and agentic decomposition. Crucially, they require selective human oversight in high-stakes scenarios.

Future research must prioritize robustness, fairness, and generalization across novel domains. Simultaneously, developers must address practical constraints regarding efficiency and privacy. Achieving genuinely trustworthy evaluation demands principled frameworks capable of adapting to complex, real-world requirements.
