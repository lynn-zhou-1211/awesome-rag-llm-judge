We often know that standard LLM evaluators will show basic biases like verbosity bias or self-enhancement bias \citep{wang2023fair}, but RAG frameworks introduce a distinct external pressure. These environmental factors act as catalysts, breaking the ability for the judge model to maintain objective neutrality. We recognize two environmental triggers which will systematically lower the reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge in RAG systems: extreme context length and unavoidable retrieval noise.

\subsection{The Context Length Foundation and Positional Bias}

In the RAG evaluation workflow, the most direct pressure is the massive volume of tokens the judge needs to process. RAG systems typically retrieve multiple lengthy documents to ground their generated answers, pushing the judge model to the very limits of its context window. Research has extensively documented the "Lost in the Middle" phenomenon, revealing that language models exhibit a severe U-shaped performance curve: they excel at extracting information from the beginning or the end of a prompt, but fail to effectively utilize the context situated in the middle \citep{lostinthemiddle2023}.


When acting as an evaluator, this positional vulnerability often translates into severe scoring biases. Recent research shows that the physical distance between related information pieces within a prompt significantly distorts the judge's perception of accuracy \citep{distance2024}. Moreover, in specific RAG evaluation environments, this positional bias is further amplified. Probing the RAG-QA arena reveals that judges often assign higher scores to responses that blindly rely on the top-ranked retrieved document, while overlooking equally valid or contradictory evidence buried deep within the context \citep{ragqa2024, posbias2025}. Although recent structural interventions—such as calibrating positional attention to improve long-context utilization \citep{foundinmiddle2024}—provide partial remedies, the fundamental fragility of handling extreme context lengths remains the primary trigger for evaluation failure.


\subsection{Distractors, Noise, and Cognitive Overload}

Beyond context length, the quality of the retrieved context introduces the second major environmental trigger: noise. Because retrieval algorithms (such as dense vector search) are fundamentally probabilistic, the resulting set of facts presented to the LLM judge is rarely perfectly clean. It is usually filled with contextual distractors—information that is semantically similar to the query, but factually unrelated and highly misleading. 

The presence of these distractors severely impairs the reasoning ability of the judge model. Recent research indicates that when reasoning models are forced to navigate through these contextual distractors, they experience catastrophic failures, a phenomenon called "Lost in the Noise" \citep{lostinnoise2026}. When evaluating RAG outputs, the judge must read the generated answer and sift through these noisy retrieved contexts to verify the faithfulness of the response. However, analysis of how retrieved context shapes internal representations reveals that distracting tokens can aggressively overwrite or distort the model's logical routing \citep{internalrep2026}. 

Consequently, an evaluator might penalize a perfectly correct answer simply because the judge itself became confused by the "insufficient" or noisy context provided in the prompt. To counter this problem, researchers are actively exploring Dynamic Context Selection methods to filter out these distractors before they reach the judge \citep{dynamiccontext2025}. However, when noise inevitably sneaks into the prompt, it forces the judge to make a critical choice: trust the noisy external context it just read, or rely on its own pre-trained memory. This specific environmental pressure point acts as the direct gateway to the core epistemological conflict discussed in the next section.
